\section{Datasets}

Experiments were conducted using the vanilla Minecraft 1.21 jar as the base
(content) pack. The evaluation corpus spans multiple target packs rather than
a single Ashen-only case study. This report compares a historical
diffusion-only sweep with the current dual-model ensemble, which combines
StyleAwareUNet, RecolorNet, deterministic recoloring, support-aware candidate
selection, and source-close loss routing. The target packs cover minimalist,
vanilla-adjacent, cartoon, fantasy, and higher-resolution stylized packs, so
the report can describe both general trends and pack-specific failure modes.

\section{Training Configuration}

Both models were trained on a single NVIDIA GPU with bfloat16 autocast. The
diffusion model (StyleAwareUNet, 7.7M parameters) was trained for 10,000 steps
with gradient accumulation of 4 (effective batch size 16), while RecolorNet
(1.0M parameters) was trained for 2,500 steps where a recolor branch was
available. Full configuration details are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:training-config}.

\section{Evolution of Training Runs}

\subsection{Diffusion Baseline}

The initial wide sweep used a diffusion-only model with a linear noise schedule
and base channel count 128. No post-hoc selector or recolor fallback was
available. The main value of this baseline is not a single headline number,
but the fact that the same model family was exposed to seven distinct pack
styles. Minimalist and vanilla-adjacent targets converged quickly: Bare Bones
and Faithful already looked coherent at their latest preview steps, while
stylized packs with stronger palette shifts such as Ashen, Cartoon, and
Chibli showed more visible drift, especially on noisy materials and fine
outlines. This broad run established that \emph{pack difficulty is not
uniform}; the training recipe that is comfortable for Bare Bones is not
automatically adequate for a 64$\times$ stylized pack compressed to
$32\times32$.

\subsection{Dual-Model Architecture}

The next iteration introduced RecolorNet as a structural fallback together
with the content-aware loss suite (structure, contrast, hue, color moment).
This reduced hue instability and helped the diffusion branch preserve stronger
edge structure, but it also made clear that a fixed routing threshold was too
crude across multiple packs. Hard textures such as \texttt{bookshelf.png} and
\texttt{tnt\_side.png} benefit from deterministic structure preservation,
whereas easy textures in minimalist packs can be handled by diffusion alone.
A pack-blind selector therefore risks being either too permissive on difficult
packs or too conservative on easy ones.

\subsection{Post-Hoc Quality Evaluation}

The next iteration replaced the fixed routing logic with the structural-style
evaluator described in Section~\ref{sec:inference}. This evaluator scores the
actual diffusion output against the content image and its support set, then
decides whether to keep diffusion, apply detail injection, or fall back to
recolor. Training improvements included EMA (decay 0.995), linear
learning-rate warmup over 5\% of steps, and an adaptive content-loss ramp over
the early phase of training.

\subsection{High-Pattern Content Gating}

The post-hoc evaluator was further refined to detect and tightly screen
\emph{high-pattern content}---textures with strong line art, symbolic glyphs,
or repeated narrow bands such as \texttt{tnt\_side.png} and
\texttt{bookshelf.png}. When the content texture exceeds thresholds on edge
energy, detail energy, and complexity, the evaluator applies a multiplicative
gate (0.55 + 0.45 $\times$ gate\_score) to the quality score and raises the
acceptance threshold to 0.68. The gate score combines tighter edge ratio
fidelity (window $[0.78, 1.18]$), detail energy ratio fidelity (window
$[0.80, 1.16]$), and a positive detail coherence gate. This prevents the
selector from accepting diffusion outputs that pass the general quality
threshold but are structurally unsuitable for precision-dependent textures.
The source-relative change agreement score was also added at this stage,
blending the support descriptor comparison with a term that rewards candidates
whose change-from-vanilla magnitude matches the support pack's typical change
level.

\subsection{Source-Close Loss Routing and Final Configuration}

The final configuration introduced SNR-weighted $x_0$ auxiliary losses, meaning
reconstruction and content losses are scaled by $\bar{\alpha}_t$, the
signal-to-noise ratio at the current diffusion timestep. This prevents the
model from over-committing to unreliable $x_0$ estimates at high noise levels.
The content loss weight was reduced to 0.20 and the content-loss components
were rebalanced to weights 1.0 / 2.5 / 3.0 / 1.5 for structure / contrast /
hue / color-moment.

The most important later correction was to route source-close targets back to a
legacy direct objective. A training sample is considered source-close when the
target/content RGB MAE is at most 0.045. Those samples use noise prediction
plus reconstruction and luminance-gradient terms only, dropping the content,
color, and per-channel gradient auxiliaries. This isolates packs and textures
that are already close to vanilla. In the current training logs, Bare Bones
routes approximately 53\% of minibatch samples through this path, while Ashen,
Cartoon, and Chibli route only about 2--5\%. The heuristic therefore captures
the easy-pack case without hard-coding a specific pack name.

Additional refinements included:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symmetric detail energy fidelity in the post-hoc evaluator.
    \item Combined candidate selection using structural and style quality scores.
    \item Wood-family routing that ranks diffusion candidates primarily by support-style agreement.
    \item Disabled detail injection for wood textures, where the injection path had introduced plank-line artifacts.
    \item Conservative RecolorNet fallback for wood, used only when diffusion is structurally broken or clearly worse.
\end{itemize}

\section{Qualitative Analysis}

\subsection{What This Model is Suited For}

\textbf{Vanilla-adjacent and minimalist packs.} Bare Bones, Faithful, and parts
of Lithos are structurally close enough to vanilla that the system can focus
on palette and contrast remapping rather than inventing new geometry. This is
why Bare Bones dominates the diffusion-only baseline and remains clean under
the current source-close routing.

\textbf{Medium-complexity stylized materials.} Ashen and Cartoon benefit from
the content-aware loss suite because they require both strong palette movement
and controlled edge placement. Wood, stone, and ore textures usually preserve
the correct large-scale structure while moving into the correct pack-specific
value range.

\textbf{Wood-family textures.} Wood and plank textures are a special case:
their quality depends on long, regular grain and plank-line continuity. The
current selector treats them as source-close style-transfer cases, skips detail
injection, and allows the best diffusion candidate to win unless it is clearly
broken. This restores the sharper diffusion-like plank structure while keeping
the recolor fallback available for genuinely failed samples.

\subsection{What This Model Struggles With}

\textbf{High-resolution packs compressed to $32\times32$.} Chibli is the
clearest example. The pack has only 87 training pairs after preprocessing and
starts from 64$\times$ artwork. Even when the diffusion candidate has
respectable overall style, the downscaled microtexture and line work remain
difficult to preserve.

\textbf{Transparent or pseudo-transparent materials.} Glass remains a
fundamental problem because alpha is discarded before modeling. The network
must hallucinate a fully opaque RGB realization of a texture whose appearance
in the target pack is often driven more by transparency treatment than by raw
color.

\textbf{Fine symbolic detail.} \texttt{tnt\_side.png} and
\texttt{bookshelf.png} remain good stress tests because they require preserving
legible letters or narrow repeated book spines. In those cases, the selector
usually prefers recolor or blend candidates over raw diffusion.

\section{Ablation Studies}

\subsection{Selection Policy Versus Oracle Error}

The cross-pack comparison reveals a useful tension: the selector is not
optimizing target-aware MAE directly. Bare Bones and Chibli both show lower
average MAE for the raw diffusion branch than for the selected output, but the
selected outputs are structurally safer and more regular. This is not
necessarily a bug; it reflects the design goal of preferring plausible,
tileable textures over target-aware oracle error. It does mean, however, that
future work should calibrate the selector separately for easy packs and hard
packs rather than using one conservative policy everywhere.

\subsection{Detail Injection Usage}

The saved selected panels show that detail injection and blend selection are
not rare corner cases. In the current checkpoint, all 18 Bare Bones selections
and 15 of 19 Cartoon selections differ from the raw diffusion preview,
indicating diffusion-derived blend candidates. On Ashen and Chibli, by
contrast, the selector often settles on the pure recolor output. This suggests
the blend path is most valuable when the pack remains close enough to vanilla
for diffusion detail to be trusted locally.

The non-destructive detail injection (Section~\ref{sec:inference}) was
critical for this result on non-wood textures. Earlier variants that rebuilt
the output from a blurred diffusion base caused global softening on already
good diffusion samples. The current approach adds only missing high-frequency
residuals where local layout agreement and detail alignment gates permit,
preserving diffusion detail that is already plausible.

\subsection{Pack-Dependent Loss Interaction}

The multi-pack comparison supports the concern raised during development that
extra structural penalties can backfire on very easy packs. Bare Bones is
already strong in the diffusion-only baseline; a flat content-loss recipe
therefore makes it optimize auxiliary targets that are less useful than direct
reconstruction. The source-close target/content MAE heuristic addresses this
without hard-coding Bare Bones: packs and textures close to vanilla use the
direct objective, while stylized packs still receive the full content-aware
loss. Ashen is the opposite case, and benefits clearly from the added structure
and hue guidance.

\section{Limitations of Current Approach}

\textbf{No alpha-channel modeling.} Despite preprocessing and storing alpha
channels in the dataset, the model operates on RGB only. Transparent textures
(glass, ice, nether portal) are inherently ill-posed in RGB space.

\textbf{Heuristic support ranking.} The descriptor-based ranking with filename
priors is effective but brittle. Packs using nonstandard naming conventions or
novel material families may receive suboptimal support assignments. A learned
ranking model could improve generalization.

\textbf{Pack-specific training.} Each target pack requires a separate training
run. While this enables pack-specific specialization (which is the project's
stated goal), it precludes zero-shot generalization to unseen packs.

\textbf{Resolution ceiling.} The fixed $32\times32$ resolution limits detail
fidelity for packs with complex microtexture (leaves, bookshelves). A
multi-resolution training scheme could address this but would increase
computational cost proportionally.

\textbf{Evaluation artifacts are preview-first.} Several runs store validation
as composite preview panels rather than as standalone metric JSON files for
every pack and every candidate. That is adequate for human inspection and for
the post-hoc analysis performed here, but a future report-oriented pipeline
should export raw per-pack metrics directly to avoid reconstruction work.
